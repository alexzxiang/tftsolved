Why it exists

The first version of my board-strength model said a three-star unit was barely stronger than a one-star one: 41 percent, then 43, then 46.

Nothing in the code looked broken. Each weight was plausible on its own, and the tests passed, because I had never asked where any single number came from. I had mistaken a model that sounded confident for one that was right. If I wanted the tool to earn trust, every number in it needed a source.

That became the rule TFTSolved is built around. A provenance audit runs with the test suite and fails if any number that moves a prediction is a guess rather than a measurement. The rest of this page is what the rule produced: four models, each checked against real games, and a short list of beliefs the data took away from me.

How it decides

Four questions, answered in order for every one of the 57 comps it knows.

  1. Where can this board go?

    A naive Bayes model reads your units against every comp's openers and final boards. Split-conformal prediction turns its odds into a short realistic set, so a full board isn't told that every comp is open.

    Keeps the true line 88.7% of the time at a 90% target, on 2,000 held-out boards.

  2. What is each line worth to you?

    Items, artifacts, emblems and augments move a line by effects measured on 22,784 Set 18 boards and 40,344 Master+ games, each with a confidence interval.

    An artifact on its usual holder is worth 0.26 places more than a normal item; on the wrong one, 0.11 less.

  3. How strong is the board?

    A Lanchester model treats a fight as total effective HP times total damage. Its weights are fitted on the last fight of 2,807 real lobbies, first place against second.

    Log-likelihood −0.609 per fight, against −0.665 on the label it replaced.

  4. Can you survive until it pays?

    An absorbing Markov chain over round and HP, solved exactly, follows each line's power curve to the round it comes online and asks how often your HP gets there.

    Game-to-game spread fitted to 19,647 Set 18 knockouts; every comp anchored to its meta average within 0.023 places.

What the data overruled

Some of my instincts as a player survived contact with the data. These did not.

  • I assumed: Best-in-slot items matter most on the main carry.

    The data: Per item, they mattered no more there than on a second carry. Main carries almost always end with good items; second carries get the leftovers.

  • I assumed: Players knocked out in the same round are a fair comparison of their boards.

    The data: Their order follows HP, not a fight between them, and that label halved every weight. The last fight of a lobby, first place against second, is the clean one.

  • I assumed: A fine-tune that improves held-out error is safe to apply.

    The data: It divided by a nearly flat slope and inflated trait weights tenfold. A unit whose boards averaged 7.4th was scored as strongly good.

  • I assumed: Placement data would show that low HP punishes fast-9 lines.

    The data: Players choose fast 9 when they're healthy, so the histograms can't show it. The answer had to come from a model of the mechanism.

A number I can't trace is just a confident guess.

Built within the rules

Riot's policies forbid tools that make suggestions from a player's live game state, so TFTSolved takes manual input and is meant for planning and post-game review.

Its Set 19 meta statistics come from MetaTFT and lolchess, whose terms keep that data private, so it stays on my machine; neither this page nor the public code contains it. The next version runs on its own data: a Riot API crawl of ranked Set 19 games once the set goes live.

A lone three-star Ziggs now beats a typical single unit 94 percent of the time, and every point of that number has a source.
